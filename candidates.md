# Candidate market: the large one-off decision

Not the business. `strategy.md` is. This is a working file, and it gets **deleted**
when the question is answered — the commit history is the record, the way medspa
was handled.

Health and children's outcomes were evaluated here and cut; both are in the
history. What is left is one shape.

**This fails P5 today.** Content comes from work actually done, and I have no
clients in it. Written down so it doesn't have to be re-derived, not so it can be
started now.

---

## The shape

An asset the buyer decides about roughly once a decade: large enough that being
wrong is catastrophic, rare enough that they research instead of habituate, and
complex enough that they will never do it themselves.

It qualifies on all four filters:

1. **Expensive and rare** — six to seven figures, once.
2. **Never do it themselves** — they must understand enough to choose well, and
   will always hire the doing.
3. **Capacity- and trust-bound** — fragmented, referral-driven, no aggregator.
4. **Prices not comparable** — two advisors can charge 5× different and both stay
   busy.

### Recurring revenue is not a filter

An earlier version killed this shape for not recurring, citing step 4. That was
out of scope — step 4 is aimed at *comparable implementation sold to the retainer
buyer*, not at high-price one-off projects. Gym Launch phase one was a one-off
project business: fly out, thirty days, ~100k USD collected, leave. Abandoned over
delivery control, not over its shape.

**What actually governs a one-off project business:**

- Churn is 100% by design, so **the lead engine is the business**. That makes this
  shape a *better* fit for content-led than a retainer is, not worse.
- Viability is price per project against cost to acquire the next one.
- Each finished project produces proof, lowering the next acquisition cost.
  **The compounding sits in acquisition, not revenue.**

Test: *can one project's price carry the acquisition cost of the next, and does
finishing it make the next cheaper?*

## Where inside it does deep content actually pay

The determinant is not size of asset. It is this:

> **Is the question they research the same question they hire on?**

In most large-asset markets it isn't. A homeowner researches *should I extend or
move*, then hires the builder their neighbour used. Research and hiring come
apart, and content reaches someone who converts elsewhere. Content only pays where
**the thing they're confused about is the thing you're paid for**.

Second determinant: **how long is the window between first worry and decision?**
Content compounds against a long window. A two-year window means one good essay
can be found, re-found, and forwarded before the decision exists.

## The recommendation: sell-side preparation, not the transaction

Within business exit, the content-rich position is **the two to three years before
the sale**, not the sale.

**Why preparation and not brokerage.** Brokerage is a success fee at the end of a
years-long process, contingent on a close I don't control, competing with
established firms on deal access. Preparation is a paid diagnostic I could do with
skills I already have: read the regnskab, establish real earnings, find
founder-dependence, measure retention, find what is actually driving the multiple.
It bills now, it repeats annually until the sale, and it puts me in the room years
before any broker.

**Hormozi's own best example of selling content is this exact topic.** His
demonstration of declarative knowledge — teach how it *works*, not how to *do* it
— is: go from 2 mio to 5 mio and you cross the institutional threshold, the
multiple goes 3 to 7, so 3 mio of extra revenue turns 4 mio of value into 35.
"People are like, whoa, that is really interesting."
[Watch This To Get Ahead In 2026, 27:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spXH1kJ6q-Q&t=1634s)

He also lays out the two levers plainly: 99% of transactions are a multiple of
EBITDA, so you influence the earnings and you influence the multiple — and the
multiple falls when the business is purely transactional, has one acquisition
channel, and has a founder inside delivery, marketing and operations.
[How the top 1% make their money, 8:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fD-sxKiB30M&t=503s)

**Market size, measured not reasoned.** Knap 40.000 danske virksomheder stand
before a generationsskifte; over 100.000 expected within ten years. Enumerable
from CVR, and `bfj.py` already reads the figures that rank them.

**The bridge from margin is real and worth naming.** For an ecommerce business,
provable contribution margin *is* a multiple driver — a buyer pays more for
earnings they can verify and less for a founder's assertion. Same competence, same
data sources, larger fee, different decision. This is the one candidate that is a
continuation rather than a switch.

### Which language the content is in

The rule that decides it: **match the language of the decision, not the language
of the topic.** The topic is universal — multiples, EBITDA, founder-dependence are
the same everywhere. The decision is not: selling a Danish ApS, under Danish tax,
to a Danish or Nordic buyer, is made in Danish by someone who will use their first
language for the most frightening financial decision of their life.

Three configurations, and only one of them is what I actually reached for.

**A. Danish content → Danish service.** The P7 puddle. Almost no competition —
few people write well about generationsskifte in Danish — and every reader who
finds it is qualified by the act of searching in Danish. Low volume, high
concentration.

**B. English content → Danish service.** *This is the one that fails, and it is
the one I was drawn to.* The audience and the buyer are different populations.
Danish SME owners facing a generationsskifte number ~40.000 against a global
English-reading business audience in the hundreds of millions. To reach them
through English I need reach in the tens of millions; to reach them in Danish I
need to reach Danes. It is the hobby-niche trap in a new costume: an audience I
cannot serve, and content whose readers convert somewhere else.

**C. English content → English service.** Jurisdiction-neutral preparation sold
internationally. Coherent, and it is the red ocean — every M&A advisor, every PE
associate and every search-fund account already publishes exactly this. P7: the
localisation cost is the moat, and this configuration throws it away. It also
throws away the CVR data advantage, which is the only proprietary thing I have.

*What I could not verify:* English proficiency in Denmark by age. EF EPI puts
Denmark at 603, #7 of 116, with regions from 584 (Syddanmark) to 631
(Midtjylland). But EF's sample is self-selected online test-takers — young, urban,
motivated — so it overstates the proficiency of a 62-year-old owner in Sønderjylland,
who is the actual buyer. Directional, not evidence.

### The split that saves the instinct

English is not wrong. It is aimed at the wrong side of the table.

**Danish to be found by sellers. English to be trusted by buyers.** Nordic M&A
runs its deal process in English — acquirers, PE, advisors, documents. And the
number-one thing that kills this business is *deal access*: if owners route to
their revisor regardless, knowledge doesn't convert. Being visible and credible to
the buy side in English is a direct lever on exactly that constraint, which no
amount of Danish seller-facing content touches.

So: two audiences, two languages, one business. Not two businesses — the content
serves one transaction from both ends.

*Hypothesis, unverified:* that Nordic buy-side operates in English by default, and
that being known to it produces deal access. Check by looking at how three
completed Danish SME sales were actually sourced.

### What kills it

Ranked by speed:

1. **Deal access, not knowledge, is the moat.** If owners route to their revisor
   or bank regardless of who taught them, content reaches people who convert
   elsewhere — the research/hire split, and it would kill the whole thesis.
   *Check first: ask three owners who sold, who they called and why.*
2. **The window is long and the revenue is lumpy.** Years from first contact to
   fee, with nothing in between unless preparation is genuinely sold as its own
   deliverable at its own price.
3. **P5 again.** No case study, and here the proof required is heavier — nobody
   hands over an exit on someone's first one.
4. **Denmark's size.** 40.000 is the pool; the addressable slice is the ones large
   enough to pay and small enough to be un-served by Beierholm and the corporate
   finance arms. `score.py` answers this the way it answered medspa.
